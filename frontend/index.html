Method

Fifteen counter sites are placed across the income fifths with synthetic true volumes. The simulated tool undercounts by 25% in low-income tracts and 20% in high-minority tracts, and overcounts by 8% in high-income tracts. A tract that is both low-income and high-minority gets both undercounts, 0.75 × 0.80 = 0.60, which is why the poorest fifth shows about −40%.

Those sizes are assumptions. The direction follows Roy et al. (2019), who found Strava counts rise about 12% for each 10-point rise in a tract's white population share. Replace the simulated estimates with a vendor's tract-level output and real counter data to measure the actual gap.

Method

Need is each tract's NCDOT pedestrian and cyclist crashes per 1,000 residents a year. Each funded project fills the tract's weakest OpenStreetMap category (crossings, bike lanes, signals or traffic calming).

The simulated tool ranks tracts by 0.6 × median income + 0.4 × crash rate (both rescaled 0–1), standing in for a tool that follows where data and advocacy are densest. Swap in a real tool's project list and the same comparison measures it.

Method

Each tract's infrastructure score weights OpenStreetMap crossings, footways, cycleways, traffic signals and traffic calming per 1,000 residents. Potential demand scales with population, rising up to 1.5× in the poorest tracts for car-free households. Actual demand is potential × score², so suppression is 1 − score².

The naive detector follows observed demand, which collapses where infrastructure is missing. The infrastructure-aware detector partly corrects for the score. Both are models; neither is a vendor product.
